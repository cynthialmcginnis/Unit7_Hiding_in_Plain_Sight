A calculator app

Every rule inside was written by a person. It never learns and never guesses. Most answers draw the line here.

Gmail's spam filter

It learned what spam looks like from millions of emails people marked as spam. Nobody wrote a rule for each scam. Machine learning.

A thermostat set to 70°F

One fixed rule: below 70, heat on. A smart thermostat that learns your schedule is a different machine.

Your GPS route to the base gate

Two parts. The route itself comes from shortest-path search, the family of A*, an algorithm built in 1968 for SRI's Shakey robot project (Hart et al., 1968). It does not learn. The arrival time comes from machine learning: Google Maps and DeepMind trained graph neural networks on traffic history and improved ETAs in cities including Tokyo and Osaka (Lange & Perez, 2020).

Netflix's "Top Picks for You" row

Built from what millions of members watched, finished, and skipped. A recommender system, Unit 5.

Microsoft Copilot

A large language model that writes by predicting the next probable token. GenAI, Unit 3.

Stop 1: Where the idea came from

AI is older than most people think, and its history runs in booms and busts. Flip each card.

1950: Can a machine think?
Tap to flip
Alan Turing proposed a test: if you can't tell a machine's answers from a person's, does the difference matter? The argument he started is still running (Turing, 1950).
1956: The field gets a name
Tap to flip
John McCarthy's summer workshop at Dartmouth coined "artificial intelligence." The organizers expected major progress in one summer (Taulli, 2019).
1970s and late 1980s: Two AI winters
Tap to flip
Promises outran results. Funding collapsed after Britain's 1973 Lighthill Report, then again when expert systems proved too costly to maintain (Taulli, 2019).
2012: Deep learning wins
Tap to flip
A deep neural network trained on GPUs crushed the ImageNet image contest (Krizhevsky et al., 2012). More data and more computing power revived a decades-old idea.
2022: GenAI goes public
Tap to flip
ChatGPT reached the public in November 2022. For the first time, most people used an AI system they could talk to.

Stop 2: Who writes the rules?

The core shift in this course fits in one diagram. Switch between the two and watch which box moves.

Rules people write+Data→Answers

A tax calculator. A programmer types in every bracket and every exception. When the law changes, a person rewrites the rules.

Data+Answers people labeled→Rules the machine learns

A spam filter. You feed it thousands of emails already marked spam or not spam. It finds the patterns itself, and nobody can list every rule it learned.

Why it matters: a spam filter can catch a scam email nobody has ever seen, because it learned the pattern. But nobody can read the rules it learned, so when it blocks a real email, no one can say exactly why.

Deep learning sits inside machine learning: it is machine learning with many-layered neural networks.

Stop 4: Seeing and practicing

Computer vision

You see a 7. The model sees a grid of numbers from 0 (black) to 255 (white).

A vision model learns which patterns of numbers mean "7," or "pedestrian," or "weed." Early layers find edges, later layers find shapes, the last layers name objects.

Reinforcement learning

No answer key. So how does it learn?
Tap to flip
An agent tries an action, gets a reward or a penalty, and adjusts. Thousands of tries later it has a strategy nobody wrote. AlphaGo learned Go partly by playing itself (Silver et al., 2016). Your Unit 4 grid-world demo did the same thing on a small maze.
What goes wrong?
Tap to flip
The agent chases the reward you wrote, not the goal you meant. Reward the wrong thing and it finds a loophole.

Introducing Bank of America's Erica

An application of AI in banking.

What it is. Erica is the virtual assistant inside Bank of America's mobile app, launched in 2018. You type a request, and Erica pulls up account details, moves money, or connects you to a person.

The problem it solved. Customers kept calling the bank for simple things, like ordering checks, because the features were buried three menus deep on a five-inch screen (Crosman, 2026).

The AI it uses. Natural language processing and machine learning read your message and match it to a set of prepared answers. The bank states that Erica does not use GenAI (Bank of America, n.d.).

By the numbers

More than 3.2 billion interactions since 2018 (Bank of America, 2026).

24.6 million active users in the second quarter of 2026, up 23% from a year earlier (Yahoo Finance, 2026).

How they know, part 1

Netflix
The rows on your home screen, ordered just for you

Recommender system (Unit 5)
It learns from what millions of members play, finish, and abandon, then ranks titles for each person. Netflix engineers reported that recommendations influence about 80% of the hours members stream.
Gomez-Uribe & Hunt (2015)
Waymo
Paid rides with nobody in the driver's seat
Tap to see how it knows
Computer vision plus robotics (Units 4 and 6)
Lidar, radar, and 29 cameras on its Jaguar I-PACE vehicles feed perception models that label cars, cyclists, and pedestrians. Prediction models forecast what each will do, and a planner picks the path. Waymo says all three stages use machine learning. Sense, plan, act at street scale.
Waymo (2021); Waymo (n.d.)
Google Translate
A sign in Japanese becomes English on your phone
Tap to see how it knows
NLP: neural machine translation (Unit 3)
Since 2016, a neural network trained on millions of human-translated sentence pairs translates whole sentences at once instead of phrase by phrase. Idioms and local slang still trip it up, for the reason you saw at Stop 3: it predicts likely words, it doesn't know what you meant.
Wu et al. (2016)

How they know, part 2

AlphaFold
The 3D shape of a protein, predicted from its recipe
Tap to see how it knows
Deep learning (Unit 2)
Trained on protein structures scientists had already solved in the lab, it predicts a protein's shape from its amino acid sequence. It has predicted structures for virtually all of the roughly 200 million known proteins. Its creators shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
Jumper et al. (2021); Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences (2024)
Amazon's robot fleet
A million warehouse robots that must not collide
Tap to see how it knows
Robotics plus GenAI (Units 6 and 3)
Amazon's millionth robot went to a fulfillment center in Japan in 2025. A GenAI foundation model called DeepFleet, trained on the fleet's own movement data, coordinates robot traffic. Amazon says it cuts robot travel time by 10%; that is the company's own figure.
Amazon (2025); Evans (2025)
Microsoft Copilot
The assistant built into Microsoft 365
Tap to see how it knows
Large language model, GenAI (Unit 3)
It predicts the next probable token from patterns in its training text. It can pull in web results or your files, but it still writes by prediction, so it can state a wrong thing fluently. Compare Erica: same banking question, one system you can audit, one you can't fully predict.

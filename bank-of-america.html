Case study in enterprise AI

Bank of America’s Internal AI Strategy

How a regulated organization built, governed, and scaled specialized AI systems—without treating every problem as a generative-AI chatbot problem.

Central question: When does an organization build or tightly control AI internally, and what does responsible deployment look like?

Reported in 2025
90%+

of Bank of America employees reportedly used its AI-driven virtual assistant.

Internal AI can mean a system of people, data, controls, and workflows—not merely a model.

Speaker prompt: Ask students to name an AI system they use. Then ask: “Do you think it was built as a single model, or as a larger system?”

First distinction

“Internal AI” is not one thing

Bank of America’s story involves several related systems serving customers and employees. The best-known name is Erica, but the strategy is broader than a consumer chatbot.

C

Customer assistant

Erica helps retail clients navigate account information, transactions, spending insights, and selected service tasks.

E

Employee assistant

Erica for Employees supports internal self-service: technology help, HR information, and navigation of enterprise processes.

G

Generative AI uses

More recent, bounded uses include research synthesis, coding assistance, meeting preparation, and training simulations.

Key idea: the bank did not replace all systems with one open-ended AI. It matched different AI approaches to different tasks and risk levels.

How the strategy began

Start with friction—not with the technology

Bank of America faced a familiar digital-service challenge: its mobile app had many features, but users could still struggle to find the right one. The design problem was how to let people state a goal in ordinary language rather than search through complex menus.

2017

Product problem recognized

Designers focused on navigation friction in an increasingly feature-rich mobile-banking experience.

2018

Erica launches

The virtual assistant becomes a conversational entry point to selected customer tasks and information.

2020

Employee adaptation

A related assistant expands internal self-service and help-desk support.

2025+

Expansion and reuse

AI capabilities are extended across business functions, with more targeted generative-AI applications.

Speaker prompt: Contrast “We need a chatbot” with “Our users cannot efficiently locate or complete task X.” Which is a stronger problem statement?

Build, buy, or assemble?

What “built internally” actually means

BofA’s approach was not necessarily to invent every AI component from zero. It exercised internal ownership over the specialized product layer: the data connections, user experience, workflow rules, permissions, safety controls, and governance.

Likely owned and customized

  • Banking vocabulary, intents, policies, and service flows
  • Connections to authorized internal systems and APIs
  • Authentication, permissions, logging, and escalation pathways
  • User experience and quality testing in a banking context
  • Risk, legal, accessibility, and security controls

Could be sourced or reused

  • Open-source or external NLP capabilities
  • Existing enterprise HR and IT service platforms
  • Cloud infrastructure and specialized development tools
  • Foundation-model capability for tightly bounded GenAI cases

A realistic enterprise-AI definition: a model becomes useful only when it is embedded in a controlled system of data, permissions, interfaces, processes, and people.

Why internal control mattered

Why not simply use a general chatbot?

1

Domain fit

Banking terms, products, rules, and client needs require specialized language and carefully designed intents.

2

Data protection

Financial information is sensitive. Access must be authenticated, authorized, and limited to what a user should see.

3

Reliability

A plausible but false response can create consumer harm, compliance risk, and loss of trust.

4

Workflow action

The assistant needs to connect to real systems—not merely produce text—to guide or complete legitimate tasks.

5

Auditability

A regulated firm needs to inspect, test, document, and improve behavior over time.

6

Reuse at scale

Core capabilities can be adapted for employees, advisors, customer service, and training while preserving controls.

A crucial technical-literacy idea

Erica is not simply “ChatGPT for banking”

Bank of America has described Erica’s core customer-facing experience as machine-learning and natural-language processing used to identify a user’s intent and select suitable, predefined responses. It has stated that this is not the same as an open-ended large language model.

Design issueConstrained assistantOpen-ended generative assistant
BehaviorRecognizes known intents and follows tested response/workflow pathsGenerates new language and can address a wider range of phrasing
StrengthPredictability, consistency, and easier testing for routine tasksFlexibility, synthesis, drafting, and conversational exploration

Teaching move: Ask students which failure they would prefer in a banking context: “I can’t do that” or a confident but incorrect answer. Why?

The non-model layer

Enterprise AI is a socio-technical system

The visible conversation is only one layer. Responsible AI performance depends on the whole system around it.

Data and technical layers

  • Current, well-structured, authorized data sources
  • APIs and system integrations
  • Identity verification and role-based access
  • Monitoring, testing, and incident response
  • Version control and documentation

Human and organizational layers

  • UX research and accessibility review
  • Legal, compliance, privacy, and security input
  • Human handoff for ambiguity or higher-risk cases
  • Training for employees and users
  • Measures of quality, fairness, and user impact

Useful mental model

Practical AI value = model capability + reliable data + workflow integration + human oversight + user trust.

Governance and accountability

Responsible design requires more than accuracy

BofA has described an enterprise governance approach involving senior business, strategy, and risk leadership, with attention to explainability, bias, intellectual property, guardrails, performance measurement, return on investment, and human involvement.

Questions before launch

What task is the system permitted to do? What data can it access? What errors are unacceptable?

Questions during use

How do users verify output? When is the system required to hand off to a person? Are outcomes uneven across groups?

Questions after launch

What is monitored? Who investigates harms? How are models, prompts, data, and policies updated?

High adoption or lower call volume is evidence of operational use—not conclusive evidence that a system is fair, accessible, accurate, or beneficial for everyone affected.

In-class application · 10–15 minutes

Design the “Campus Erica”

Your university wants an AI assistant to reduce friction for students. Choose one narrow use case: locating tutoring, finding a financial-aid deadline, resetting a password, checking library hours, or interpreting a course schedule.

  1. State the exact user problem in one sentence.
  2. Identify the approved source(s) of information.
  3. Decide what the system may do—and what it must never do.
  4. Choose: predefined responses, retrieval plus GenAI, or human-only support. Explain why.
  5. Name one unacceptable error.
  6. Design the human handoff: when, to whom, and with what information?
  7. Identify one privacy, accessibility, or fairness risk.
  8. Propose three success measures beyond “number of users.”

Debrief: Is an assistant that handles fewer tasks safely better than a broad assistant that sometimes improvises?

Takeaway

What this case teaches about AI

Bank of America’s strategy illustrates that successful organizational AI is not only about deploying the most powerful model. It is about selecting appropriate tasks, connecting trustworthy data, constraining high-risk behavior, involving people, and continuously governing the system.

For nontechnical AI literacy

  • Differentiate NLP, machine learning, and generative AI.
  • Recognize build-versus-buy as a governance decision.
  • Evaluate AI systems beyond impressive demos.

Questions to carry forward

  • What task is actually being automated?
  • What data and permissions make it possible?
  • Who is accountable when the system is wrong?
